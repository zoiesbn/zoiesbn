Why “More Options” Can Hurt Conversion

An extra payment option can help someone finish a purchase. It can also send them into another task with no clear way back.

By Zoha Sobhani · August 26, 2026 · 4 min read

Imagine reaching checkout and discovering that your account credit won't cover the purchase. The page offers an “Increase Credit” button. It sounds helpful, but what happens after you press it?

You might have to leave checkout, make another payment, wait for the balance to update, and find the original purchase again. The option solves one problem while asking you to complete several more steps.

Before adding that button, I'd want to understand the whole route back to the purchase.

Check whether the option can be used now

An option is useful when someone understands what it does and can act on it. In checkout, they also need to know what will happen to the purchase they've already started.

A customer may be in a hurry or unsure why a payment method isn't available. Explain the reason in terms they can use. Then offer a next step that works for their account and situation.

A balance check has its own constraints

Consider a product that lets customers pay from account credit. Showing the remaining balance could help them choose a payment method and understand why credit is unavailable.

But the product may need an extra backend request to fetch that balance. The result might arrive slowly or be unavailable for some account types. The team needs to decide how checkout behaves while it waits and what happens if the request fails.

These are questions to investigate in the product you're working on. A balance display that works well in one system may need different handling in another.

Follow the detour all the way back

For a postpaid customer, an “Increase Credit” action might lead to settling a bill through a bank gateway. After payment, they may still need to wait for confirmation before returning to the original purchase.

Draw those steps alongside checkout. Check what the product remembers and what the customer has to do again.

  • Does the original purchase remain available?
  • Is there a clear way back after the payment?
  • What happens if the balance hasn't updated yet?
  • Can someone recover if they close the page halfway through?

Without those answers, the extra action could leave someone with a paid bill and an unfinished purchase. That's a reason to review the journey before treating the button as an improvement.

Choose where the extra action belongs

Sometimes it makes sense to keep checkout focused on payment methods the customer can use immediately. Account management may be a better place for an action that takes them through several unrelated steps.

But removing the action isn't always the answer. If adding credit is the only practical way to complete the purchase, hiding that route could leave the customer stuck. In that case, the return to checkout deserves as much design attention as the top-up itself.

I'd compare both approaches with the team and try the full task with users. Look for confusion, repeated work, and lost progress. If the product tracks checkout completion, use that evidence to assess the change rather than assuming fewer options will improve conversion.

Before adding another button

Start with the customer's current task and follow each proposed option to its end. Check whether it helps them finish, whether it works now, and whether the same issue could be addressed earlier.

For the credit example, I'd want to see the return screen after payment before signing off on the checkout button. That screen tells us whether we've offered a usable route or simply moved the unresolved problem somewhere else.
